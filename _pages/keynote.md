<h4>Machine reasoning under epistemic uncertainty: Bayesian inference and graphical models using imprecise probabilities</h4>
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/keynote_speaker/branko_ristic.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Branko Ristic" align="left" style="border:10px solid white" width="200">
<h5><b>Branko Ristic, Principal Research Fellow</b></h5>
<h6>
RMIT University, Australia
</h6>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Abstract: </b> Machines are increasingly asked to reason over large volumes of uncertain, heterogeneous information: sensor measurements, natural-language reports such as “possible warship,” and expert domain knowledge. Much of this uncertainty is epistemic, arising from lack of knowledge rather than randomness. Standard Bayesian inference handles it by forcing imprecise knowledge into precise probabilities, and as a result it can be confidently wrong. This talk argues for integrity in machine reasoning: a model should represent exactly the knowledge available, no more and no less. We introduce imprecise probabilities, where belief is represented by credal sets (closed convex sets of probability distributions). This framework includes belief functions, possibility theory, and random sets as special cases. We show how Walley’s generalised Bayes rule can be computed in practice using bisection and bilinear programming. We then lift it to large models through credal valuation networks, a valuation algebra that supports local computation. Examples range from classification with an imprecise confusion matrix to ongoing threat assessment onboard an underwater vehicle. In these examples, the resulting probability bounds always contain the ground truth and are tighter than those of evidential networks. We close with the computational cost and open problems.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Biography: </b> Branko Ristic received the Ph.D. degree from Queensland University of Technology in 1995. After research and engineering positions in the former Yugoslavia and in Australia, he joined the Defence Science and Technology Organisation (now Defence Science and Technology Group, DSTG) in 1996. In 2003–04 he spent a year of study leave at IRIDIA, Université libre de Bruxelles, Belgium. Since 2015 he has been a Principal Research Fellow in the School of Engineering at RMIT University, where he has led some 20 research projects, mainly funded by DSTG. He is co-author of two books, Beyond the Kalman Filter: Particle Filters for Tracking Applications (Artech House, 2004) and Particle Filters for Random Set Models (Springer, 2013), as well as over 120 journal articles and 10 book chapters. His research interests include information fusion, Bayesian estimation, target tracking, Monte Carlo methods, search techniques, sensor control, and machine reasoning under uncertainty.
</p>

<h4>Excursions into Distributed and Modular Quantum Computing</h4>
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/keynote_speaker/seng_loke.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Seng Loke" align="left" style="border:10px solid white" width="200">
<h5><b>Seng Loke, Professor</b></h5>
<h6>
Deakin University, Australia
</h6>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Abstract: </b> The talk will introduce the idea of Distributed and Modular Quantum Computing, discuss some current trends in quantum computing, and provide an overview of a range of topics for research in Distributed and Modular Quantum Computing, as well as Quantum Internet Computing. In particular, the talk will discuss the potential of distributed and modular quantum computing to help overcome scalability challenges faced by current quantum computing architectures. It will briefly review advances in quantum computation, and the interconnection of quantum processors. The talk will then examine a range of emerging research directions in Distributed and Modular Quantum Computing, including distributed quantum operations, entanglement-assisted computation, quantum communication protocols, and techniques for coordinating computational tasks across multiple quantum processing units. The talk will also discuss the notion of the Quantum Internet and introduce Quantum Internet Computing, highlighting its relationship with Distributed Quantum Computing and its potential to enable new computational capabilities through networked quantum resources.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Biography: </b> Seng Loke is Professor in Computer Science at the School of Information Technology, Deakin University, Australia. He currently coordinates the Deakin University Quantum Computing Research Network (DQCRN), a Deakin University wide network focusing on quantum computing and its applications. Previously, he also served as an Adjunct Professor in both the School of Engineering and Mathematical Sciences at La Trobe University, and the School of Business and Tourism at Southern Cross University. Seng Loke's research interests revolve around the concept of the Internet-of-Things, ranging from distributed systems, smart/AI things and smarter cities to cooperative vehicles, edge computing, pervasive computing, IoT-assisted Agetech, ethics for smart devices, distributed quantum computing, and quantum Internet computing. He has more than 350 research publications, including books: “The Automated City: Internet of Things and Ubiquitous Artificial Intelligence” (Springer, 2021) co-authored with A. Rakotonirainy, and “From Distributed Quantum Computing to Quantum Internet Computing: An Introduction” (Wiley, Nov 2023).
</p>

<h4>Machine Unlearning: Ideas and Strategies</h4>
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/keynote_speaker/wanlei_zhou.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Wanlei Zhou" align="left" style="border:10px solid white" width="200">
<h5><b>Wanlei Zhou, Professor</b></h5>
<h6>
City University of Macau, Macao SAR, China
</h6>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Abstract: </b> Over the past decade, machine learning has experienced remarkable advancements, positively impacting our daily lives. Alongside this progress, a distinctive requirement has emerged—stemming from the AI era, in particular, from privacy concerns, usability considerations, and “the right to be forgotten”—leading to the development of a new concept known as machine unlearning. In this talk, I will first give a comprehensive exploration of machine unlearning and the reasons behind the existence of this necessity. Then, I will describe the methods and technologies used in machine unlearning. Third, I will talk about developments in this field, especially through a number of our recent work on machine unlearning as examples.
</p>

<h4>Data Mining and Machine Learning for Analysis of Network Traffic</h4>
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/keynote_speaker/ljiljana_trajkovic.png' | relative_url }}" alt="Ljiljana Trajkovic" align="left" style="border:10px solid white" width="200">
<h5><b>Ljiljana Trajkovic, Professor</b></h5>
<h6>
Simon Fraser University, Canada
</h6>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Abstract: </b> Collection and analysis of data from deployed networks is essential for understanding communication networks. Hence, data mining and statistical analysis of network data have been employed to determine traffic loads, analyze patterns of users' behavior,  predict future network traffic, and detect traffic anomalies. The Internet has historically been prone to failures and attacks that significantly degrade its performance, affect the Internet connectivity, and cause routing disconnections. Frequent cases of various cyber threats have been encountered over the years and, hence, detection of anomalous behavior is a topic of great interest in cybersecurity. In described case studies, traffic traces collected by various collection sites are used to classify network anomalies. Various anomaly and intrusion detection approaches based on machine learning have been employed to analyze collected data. Deep learning, broad learning, gradient boosted decision trees, and reservoir computing algorithms were used to develop models based on collected datasets that contain Internet worms, viruses, power outages, ransomware events, router misconfigurations, Internet Protocol hijacks, and infrastructure failures in times of conflict. The reported results indicate that while performance of machine learning models greatly depends on the used datasets, they are viable tools for detecting the Internet anomalies.
</p>

Ing. degree from University of Pristina, Yugoslavia, the M.Sc. degrees in electrical engineering and computer engineering from Syracuse University, Syracuse, NY, and the Ph.D. degree in electrical engineering from University of California at Los Angeles. Her research interests include communication networks and dynamical systems. <h4>Frontiers in Specialised AI: From Vision-Language Data Pipelines to DRL-Based Compiler Optimisation</h4>
<img src="{{ '/assets/images/keynote_speaker/sahraoui_dhelim.jpg' | relative_url }}" align="left" style="border:10px solid white" width="200">
<h5><b>Sahraoui Dhelim, Assistant Professor</b></h5>
<h6>
Dublin City University, Ireland
</h6>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Abstract: </b> As artificial intelligence shifts from broad foundation models toward specialized engineering solutions, custom AI architectures are reshaping how we address domain-specific computational challenges. This keynote explores the power and adaptability of specialized machine learning paradigms across two distinct operational frontiers: automated multimodal dataset curation and low-level system optimization.
<br/>
First, we examine the deployment of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to unlock unstructured historical artifacts by automating the digitization, transcription, and semantic tagging of complex Arabic manuscripts. We demonstrate how VLMs handle intricate calligraphic styles, diacritics, and physical degradation to synthesize high-fidelity datasets at scale. Second, we pivot to systems engineering, illustrating how Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) navigates the massive search spaces inherent in compiler optimization, specifically phase ordering and instruction scheduling. DRL agents learn performance heuristics that consistently outpace traditional compiler passes.
<br/>
By examining both domains through the lens of high-dimensional, irregular decision spaces, this talk presents actionable methodologies for designing resilient, domain-tailored AI pipelines across data-centric and compute-centric infrastructure.
</p>

<b>Biography: </b> Dr. Sahraoui Dhelim is the research director of the School of Computing at Dublin City University. Previously, he was a senior researcher at University College Dublin from 2021 to 2024 and a Visiting Researcher at Ulster University from 2020 to 2021.

<h4>Federated Learning-Driven O-RAN Security for 6G Networks and Comprehensive Analysis</h4>
<img src="/2025/assets/images/keynote_speaker/victor_chang.jpg" align="left" style="border:10px solid white" width="200">
<h5><b>Victor Chang, Professor</b></h5>
<h6>
Aston University, United Kingdom
</h6>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Abstract: </b>The cybersecurity issues of 6G networks and Open Radio Access Networks (O-RAN) are challenging for traditional centralized defense techniques to handle. This keynote introduces a UK-Japan partnership that combines neuromorphic AI, federated learning, and O-RAN edge deployment for near-real-time, privacy-preserving malware detection. We address privacy issues, data heterogeneity, and communication delay in federated learning with our new FedAvgVChang algorithm. In running 20 rounds, the system achieves 96.8% detection accuracy, which is 38% faster than normal federated averaging. It is tested on the CIC IoT 2023 dataset and a synthetic IoT dataset that mimics CIC IoT 2023 with simulated attack scenarios (e.g., Mirai, DDoS). On normal hardware, it achieves a median inference delay of 0.15 seconds; on neuromorphic platforms (emulated Loihi), it can achieve inference times of less than 0.8 seconds. Strong detection across a variety of attack vectors is demonstrated by performance indicators such as a global F1-score of 0.968, AUC of 0.974, precision of 0.966, and recall of 0.970. This work validates the viability of scalable, privacy-conscious threat detection in synthetic edge and O-RAN sandbox scenarios. The presentation covers system design, performance metrics, collaborative roadmap, and future goals for federated neuromorphic security in forthcoming global 6G ecosystems.
</p>

<h4>Testing-Based Formal Verification for Software Dependability</h4>
<img src="/2025/assets/images/keynote_speaker/shaoying_liu.jpg" align="left" style="border:10px solid white" width="200">
<h5><b>Shaoying Liu, Professor</b></h5>
<h6>
Software Engineering Institute of East China Normal University, China
</h6>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Abstract: </b>Software dependability encompasses five critical properties: reliability, safety, integrity, availability, and maintainability. Ensuring these attributes throughout the software engineering process remains a significant and ongoing challenge. In this talk, I will begin by briefly outlining a general framework for developing dependable software. I will then focus on an advanced technique known as Testing-Based Formal Verification (TBFV), which integrates specification-based testing with formal methods to verify program correctness. TBFV is characterized by its ability to automatically verify the correctness of all program paths explored during testing, while also having the potential to uncover previously untested paths. This dual capability supports both validation and verification, enhancing the overall assurance of software quality. I will explain the core principles of TBFV, specific techniques it employs for fault prevention, validation, and verification, as well as the key challenges associated with its practical implementation. When fully realized, TBFV is expected to significantly reduce testing time and cost, and substantially improve software dependability.
</p>
<p style="text-align: justify;">
<b>Biography: </b> Shaoying Liu is a Professor at Software Engineering Institute of East China Normal University, China, IEEE Fellow, BCS Fellow, and AAIA Fellow. He received his Ph.D. in Computer Science from the University of Manchester, U.K., in 1992, and has held research and teaching positions at ten universities in China, the United Kingdom, and Japan, respectively. His research interests include Formal Engineering Methods, Specification-based Program Inspection and Testing, Testing-Based Formal Verification (TBFV), Human Machine Pair Programming (HMPP), and Dependable Computing. Liu is a pioneer and leading researcher in Formal Engineering Methods for Software Development. He founded the ICFEM conference in 1997, SOFL+MVSL workshop in 2012, and SFPVV symposium in 2024, respectively. He designed the SOFL (Structured Object-Oriented Formal Language) specification language and method, authored two books titled "Formal Engineering for Industrial Software Development" and “Agile SOFL: Agile Formal Engineering Method”, respectively, both published by Springer, more than 15 edited books, and over 300 papers in refereed journals and international conferences. He has received many awards, including “Special Achievement Award” from IEEE DSA 2024, 2022 and 2020 “Distinguished Research Awards” from IPSJ/SIGSE respectively, the “20 Year ICFEM Impact Award” from ICFEM 2018, “IEEE Reliability Society Japan Joint Chapter 2016 Best Paper Award”, and “Outstanding Paper Award’’ from ICECCS’96. In recent years, he has served as the General/Conference Chair of several international conferences, including ICFEM 2024, WSSE 2025, and SFPVV 2025.
</p>
